    Optional

    Leave these blank to skip them. A barometer lets it remove barometric effects from the record. The earth tide needs latitude, longitude and the file clock together.

    Efficiencies depend on the units, so check them first. Barometric efficiency is 1 minus loading efficiency. To avoid pumping or sensor changes, set the fit dates to a quiet period. The result still covers the whole record.

    Enter the pump's true times from the field log. A distant well reacts late, so the level can mislead. Results scale directly with the rate. Barometric efficiency is fitted only on the record before pumping. On less than a day of that, enter your own, which the well record tool can fit from a longer record. The fit window sets which part of pumping the Theis curve and straight line use.

      Aquifer results

      Each method assumes more than the data can confirm. Agreement between them is a good sign, not proof. T, raw is the same analysis with nothing removed.

        Extended drawdown: if pumping had continued

        After the pump stops, each recovery reading gives the drawdown that would have followed had pumping continued, by the method of van der Kamp (1989). The dots right of the dashed line come from those readings. The two lines are the standard methods, carried forward from the pumping data alone.

        If the dots follow the lines, recovery supports the standard answer. If they part smoothly, the aquifer is probably not behaving as those methods assume. If the dots jump at the dashed line or turn downward, a condition of the test probably failed, so check that the static level and pumping rate held steady.

        How this was tested, and what it can't tell you

        No pumping test comes with a known true answer, so none of this shows a result is right for your test. Please judge it on your own data.

        Method. These are the published methods, in my own implementation. They have not been compared with AQTESOLV or AquiferTest output. It has not yet been run on a real test that had a barometer or recovery data.

        Real textbook data. On Oude Korendijk, from Kruseman and de Ridder (1994), the Theis fit gives T 480 m²/d and S 1.14 × 10⁻⁴, matching the same fit in TTim. A line through everything u allows gives T 491 m²/d, not the book's 385, because the book's line follows only the readings from 1.4 to 13.1 minutes, before the curve flattens. With Fit from 1.4 and Fit to 13.1 the Cooper and Jacob line gives T 385 m²/d and S 1.69 × 10⁻⁴, the book's values. With Fit to 13, the 13.1 minute reading is left out and the line gives T 382 m²/d. The fit window is a judgment, and the page warns when a line bends like this.

        Barometric correction. One invented test, T 250 m²/d and a barometric efficiency of 0.35, was moved through a real barometer record in 5-day steps, 23 start dates in all, with no tuning. The median error in T from the recovery line fell from 5.5% to 0.8%, and the worst from 27% to 2.5%. For the straight line it fell from 4.6% to 1.2%, and the worst from 19% to 2.5%. This flatters the method, because the invented well answers air pressure at once and the same way throughout, which is what the correction assumes.

        Extended drawdown. It rebuilds computed curves to within 0.3%, including one with a recharge boundary and past two pumping periods. It has not been checked against the published British Columbia tests.

        Known limits. One constant rate and one well at a time. No correction for leakage, boundaries, partial penetration or dewatering. On under 14 days before pumping only a constant efficiency is fitted, and under a day nothing is. Those cut-offs, and the warning limits, are choices of mine.

        How this was tested, and what it can't tell you

        No public well record comes with a known true answer, so nothing below shows that a correction is right for your well. Please judge it on your own data.

        Method. The standard path here is the published one: first-difference regression for constant barometric efficiency, regression deconvolution with a fixed 48-hour lag, and a lagged regression on a computed earth tide. It is my own implementation. It has not been compared with BETCO or KGS BRF output.

        Test. The plan and code were fixed before the data was downloaded. It covered 13 USGS wells that played no part in building this tool, 6 in the Klamath basin and 7 on the Virginia Coastal Plain, 5 of those at one site. Each was fitted on its first 70% and scored on the last 30%, once. No well was dropped.

        Real wells. The score is the share of typical 24-hour change removed. It is not accuracy, because it would also rise if real signal were removed. The median was 15% for the standard method and 0% for constant barometric efficiency. The standard method made this score worse than the raw record on 4 of the 13 wells, by 18 to 45%, and constant efficiency on 6 of them.

        Known signals. Signals of known size were added to quiet stretches of those wells, using a barometer the well never felt. Across eight scenarios the median error was 5.3 mm for the standard method and 12.2 mm for constant efficiency, against signals of 10 to 44 mm rms. When the true response was instant, constant efficiency did better, 1.2 mm against 4.4 mm, because 49 fitted lags cost something on a short record. I chose those scenarios, so they are a check, not field accuracy.

        Known limits. On the Virginia wells the tide fit gained the most, but those wells are near tidal water, so part of what it removed may be ocean loading, not earth tide. The computed tide is for a rigid earth and hasn't been checked against ETERNA or TSoft. Coarse barometers bias efficiencies low. The two methods can disagree widely: on some Virginia wells the fits gave 0.54 and 0.04, and there is no truth to settle which is right.
